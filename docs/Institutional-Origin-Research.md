# Institutional Origin: Historical and Folklore Candidates

[Project index](../README.md) · [Institutional decisions](Order-and-Colonial-Company.md#confirmed-institutional-origin-and-development) · [Project status](Project-Status.md)

November 5, 2026. **Open research and creative proposals.** The user's institutional selection is authoritative on the linked decisions page. No candidate crisis, supernatural explanation, founding event or connection to the historical Hospitallers is approved here.

## Purpose and boundaries

Identify substantial, dangerous European traditions or recorded crises that could motivate a fictional supernatural branch associated with the Order of St John. The user favors the English suppression of 1540 as a possible organizational turning point. That was a particular event within the wider Reformation, not the date of the whole movement.

This was a bounded comparative search across English, French, Spanish/Navarrese, Germanic and adjacent European material, not an exhaustive survey. Modern institutions, specialist scholarship, translated primary passages and catalogue descriptions were consulted in the portions identified below. No original manuscript or complete monograph was read.

A historical record can establish that an extraordinary explanation was reported without establishing its supernatural truth. A real disaster and a legend later attached to it are separate evidence. Making a supernatural cause real in the game would be a creative decision.

## Chronology and clarified research purpose

The hospital community in Jerusalem predates its papal recognition of February 15, 1113. The Order of Malta's [institutional history](https://www.orderofmalta.int/history/11-century-to-the-present-day/) places the community in the second half of the eleventh century; [its British association](https://www.orderofmalta.org.uk/history/1048-to-present/) uses the conventional date around 1048. Gerard led the community into its recognized form; 1113 is not the opening date of a personal clinic.

The late-twelfth-century revenant narratives postdate recognition. Hellequin's reported 1091 encounter predates it; connecting the Jerusalem community to that Norman account would require invented people and transmission, not merely an earlier founding date.

The initial research objective was to explain unusual appearance and modest physical advantages. The current design focuses on [autonomous revived operators with a largely human appearance](Order-and-Colonial-Company.md#revenant-operators--open). The rite and its effects remain open. The historical leads below concern why the researchers began, what they investigated, and how their work might have developed.

In the historical accounts below, “revenant” describes a returning dead person, particularly a bodily return. The game's specific use of the term is defined in the [operator design](Order-and-Colonial-Company.md#revenant-operators--open). The accounts report separate local incidents, sometimes associated with illness.

## Candidate 1: Corporeal revenants — English and Scottish border accounts, late twelfth century

**Evidence:** William of Newburgh's *Historia rerum Anglicarum*, book V, chapters 22–24, reports returning corpses at Buckingham, Berwick, Melrose and Anantis. His Berwick account connects a roaming corpse with a subsequent epidemic despite its destruction. The stories juxtapose ecclesiastical remedies and physical intervention. These are medieval narratives of claimed events, not independently verified outbreaks of walking dead.

**Sources consulted:** [Fordham's translated primary text](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/williamofnewburgh-five.asp), selected chapters 22–24; [specialist study's published summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/haskins-society-journal-32-2020-studies-in-medieval-history/wiley-lecture-monsters-in-anglonorman-historiography-two-notes-on-william-of-newburghs-revenants/91AC0B1D639C59166EEDCB13DD330D01). Precise identification of Anantis remains outside this check.

**Creative potential — Open:** Strongest combination of recognizably dangerous folklore, public illness, responsibility for bodies and disagreement over effective intervention. It could connect care of the living with knowledge of dangerous dead. Calling these modern vampires would import assumptions the texts do not establish. Earlier reports could supply inherited knowledge, but a new crisis around 1540 would be invented.

## Candidate 2: Holy fire / mal des ardents — medieval France and wider Europe

**Evidence:** Accounts of burning illness, gangrene and sometimes death became associated with saints, relics and healing institutions. A severe northern French wave is reported for 1128–1129. Ergot poisoning supplies a real medical explanation for important outbreaks; not every historical use of a saint's disease-name is a secure retrospective diagnosis.

**Sources consulted:** François Dolbeau, [edition/study of a Geneviève miracle account](https://www.persee.fr/doc/jds_0021-8103_1983_num_1_1_1464), opening discussion and indexed continuation; [Saint-Antoine museum's institutional history](https://musees.isere.fr/page/musee-de-saint-antoine-labbaye-le-site-historique), text; [Wellcome's scientific image description](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/zshwp85w), symptoms and Antonite treatment context. The full critical edition was not examined.

**Chronological clarification:** This was a recurring affliction, not one event with a single start/end date. Charles Vulliez's [study of early miracle narratives](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bsnaf_0081-1181_2018_num_2015_1_12463), opening page, identifies reports in 945 (Paris region) and 994 (Aquitaine). The 1128–1129 wave is a later example. Jacques Battin's [medical-history study](https://www.academie-medecine.fr/le-feu-saint-antoine-ou-ergotisme-gangreneux-et-son-iconographie-medievale/) discusses continuing treatment and imagery, including Gersdorff's 1517 Strasbourg surgical manual. These examples span the tenth through early sixteenth centuries without establishing uninterrupted outbreaks or identical diagnoses.

**Creative potential — Open:** Strongest hospital connection: diagnosis, feeding and protecting sufferers can require specialist work. The real Order of St Anthony already served this need; do not attribute its actual work to St John. Any genuinely supernatural affliction alongside ordinary poisoning would be original fiction, with the distinction allowing investigation and treatment beyond violence.

**Related legend:** The [Arras archive catalogue](https://ccfr.bnf.fr/portailccfr/ark:/16871/005FRCGMNOV-620416201-2Pt) recounts a foundation legend set around 1105: two enemies reconcile and receive a Marian candle to heal sufferers. The catalogue describes surviving confraternity archives from the thirteenth century onward; that does not make the miracle independently verified contemporary testimony.

## Candidate 3: Courtaut and the wolves around Paris — 1421–1439

**Evidence:** A University of Caen project reproduces passages from the *Journal d'un bourgeois de Paris*: wolves scavenging human remains, entering Paris and attacking people amid war and deprivation. It dates the capture of the feared tailless wolf Courtaut to November 1439. The familiar internet package of a 1450 red wolf leading forty deaths should not replace these dated passages.

**Source consulted:** [University of Caen, “Les loups autour de Paris”](https://mrsh.unicaen.fr/homme_et_loup/_en/cas_loups_paris.php.html), institutional synthesis and reproduced primary passages; direct opening subsequently succeeded. Original manuscript and full edition unread.

**Creative potential — Open:** Strongest grounded predatory-threat candidate. War, hunger, neglected burial and dangerous animals already connect human actions with apparent monstrosity. A supernatural predator hidden within such attacks would be an adaptation. The consulted passages do not establish that Courtaut was a werewolf or an enchanted animal.

## Candidate 4: Dancing outbreaks — Rhine region 1374; Strasbourg 1518

**Evidence:** Involuntary dancing outbreaks were recorded and could be interpreted through saints' displeasure or possession, alongside medical explanations. Strasbourg's municipal response is a documented crisis near the proposed branch date. Claims of hundreds dying or fifteen deaths daily are disputed and are not adopted.

**Sources consulted:** [Europeana's synthesis](https://www.europeana.eu/en/stories/st-johns-dance-why-cant-they-stop-dancing), historical/religious interpretation; [Strasbourg museums' 2018 publication description](https://www.musees.strasbourg.eu/edition/-/entity/id/3113559/1518%2BLa%2Bfi%C3%A8vre%2Bde%2Bla%2Bdanse), dozens of sufferers and source-critical purpose; [2023 medical article abstract](https://www.em-consulte.com/article/1586845/1518-%E2%80%93-epidemie-de-danse-mortelle-a-strasbourg), distinction between contemporaneous records and later death claims. The museum book, complete municipal records and full medical paper remain unread. Strasbourg belonged to the Holy Roman Empire in 1518; present geography is not period nationality.

**Creative potential — Open:** A substantial crisis shortly before the proposed 1540 formation. Afflicted people need protection and diagnosis; they are not automatically enemies. Any lethal supernatural mechanism would be fictional. Do not present ergot poisoning as an established explanation of the dancing outbreaks.

## Candidate 5: Navarre's witch hunt — 1525–1526

**Evidence:** The Royal and General Archive of Navarre identifies the 1525 prosecutions as a significant early wave. A university account describes the transfer from royal magistrate Balanza to inquisitorial investigation and cessation in 1526 for lack of evidence. Reports of supernatural conspiracies belong to the accusation history; persecution is the securely established violence.

**Sources consulted:** [Archive exhibition account](https://agn.navarra.es/es/exposiciones/-/asset_publisher/iJTPtY5VEwp6/content/maleficium-navarra-y-la-caza-de-brujas-siglos-xiv-xvii); [UPV/EHU research presentation](https://www.ehu.eus/es/web/zientzia-teknologia-fakultatea/-/antes-de-zugarramurdi-la-primera-caza-de-brujas-en-navarra-1525-1526-), historical summary; [2021 Avellaneda-report edition abstract](https://revistas.navarra.es/index.php/PV/article/view/1711), four versions and possible textual reworking. PDF retrieval succeeded at the introductory level but detailed follow-up failed; no complete trial or report edition was read.

**Creative potential — Open:** Strongest recent Iberian model for why supernatural investigation must distinguish evidence from blame. It could motivate a specialist ethic of finding causes while protecting accused people. Avoid making historical victims' alleged conspiracies retrospectively true or assuming all Spanish authorities behaved alike.

**Follow-up: Navarre and proposed institutional cooperation — Open**

The user favors exploring Navarre as a catalyst involving more than one organization. “Inquisitorial intervention” specifically meant the Spanish Inquisition's Calahorra tribunal and central leadership, not a generic religious investigator. Its restraint of the 1525–1526 campaign is relevant; cruelty or willingness to torture does not establish willingness to adopt condemned magic.

The [Navarre exhibition chronology](https://www.navarra.es/NR/rdonlyres/B5B1AF79-4E3E-4358-8E7A-0E09E173282A/464559/lacazadebrujasenelespacioyeneltiemposinmapa.pdf) identifies the 1539 Salazar-valley investigation and the March 16, 1540 auto-da-fé in Pamplona. Rocío de la Nogal Fernández's [archival study](https://revista-hsj-historia.unavarra.es/es/article/view/3183), pp. 375–383, provides the sequence and record references. By February 1539, royal commissioner Lope Camús was investigating around Ochagavía. Inquisitor Martín Pérez de Oliván arrived in Pamplona in December. The March ceremony's report lists 69 cases, 49 for witchcraft, including 30 children aged 10–14. It records one execution on a separate charge of Judaizing. Lope de Esparza's civil case continued after the ceremony. The author cites the confessions at AHN, Inquisición, L. 831, fols. 13–15 and the ceremony report at L. 833, fols. 12–14. Consulted the study's account and quoted passages. Original files remain uninspected.

The study describes allegations of harmful powders, renunciation of the faith and, in one confession, exhumation. These record accusations and testimony. Brief revival and any connection to Hospitaller activity belong to the fictional proposal below. The 1539–1540 proceedings were a separate episode from the 1525–1526 campaign, though the earlier proceedings appear in later testimony and the inquisitors drew on the 1526 Granada instructions.

**Historical foothold:** St John had a [Grand Priory of Navarre](https://pares.cultura.gob.es/ParesBusquedas20/catalogo/description/1698651). A [study of the Crucifijo church and hospital at Puente la Reina](https://revistas.navarra.es/index.php/PV/article/download/2114/1229) dates the transfer of the hospital to St John's Navarre priory to 1443. This gives the fictional research network a possible regional connection. Membership, patient care and specific premises for the experiments remain Open.

The user's desired fictional premise allows genuine supernatural harm in rare cases alongside false accusations and personal grievances. This does not establish any named historical defendant's guilt or a single motive behind every accusation. Fictional institutional cooperation, covert experiments, and authorization remain unresolved.

Manuel José Pedraza García's [study of Zaragoza proceedings in 1509 and 1511](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/RGID/article/view/RGID0707120063A), abstract inspected, documents inquisitorial cases involving necromancy and prohibited grimoires. It supports the existence of investigated ritual-magic practices and books, not an inquisitorial program to use them, effective supernatural powers, or a Hospitaller connection.

## Candidate 6: Hellequin's troop — Normandy, encounter dated 1091

**Evidence:** Orderic Vitalis reports the priest Walchelin's encounter with a procession of suffering dead, including armed riders. Specialist interpretation connects the account to local warfare, social wrongdoing and a broader spectral-retinue tradition. This is a reported vision recorded later, not independent proof of an army or a mass-casualty event.

**Sources consulted:** [University-linked Norman history source guide](https://mondes-normands.caen.fr/france/ensavoirplus/sources/ordericvital.htm), author and 1091 episode; [Andrew Joynes's translated-source chapter summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/medieval-ghost-stories/ecclesiastical-history-of-orderic-vitalis/C3BAF2333B5E0F1125E7ACB51F0C3FB1), narrative and historical setting. Full chapter/critical edition unread; speculative pagan derivations are not adopted.

**Creative potential — Open:** Strongest large supernatural spectacle, but weaker as evidence of a lethal regional outbreak. A procession of recognizable dead could connect warfare and unfinished obligations with danger. Do not homogenize European Wild Hunt traditions or make every apparition a hostile monster.

**User proposal — Open:** A rogue Hospitaller might investigate necromancy for healing. No individual, date, historical connection or discovery is established. A later practitioner studying an older account avoids requiring membership in the formally recognized order in 1091. Medical rescue leaving a survivor permanently altered is an exploratory possibility, not an approved induction mechanism.

## Screened limits and alternatives

- Rungholt's destruction in the 1362 storm surge is supported by archaeology. The [Kiel-led research](https://www.uni-kiel.de/en/university/details/news/135-rungholt) and [regional government's legend account](https://www.schleswig-holstein.de/DE/landesportal/land-und-leute/typisch-sh/sagen-und-legenden-aus-dem-norden/_documents/rungholt) support keeping disaster and divine-punishment narrative separate. Pre-1540 circulation of specific surviving legend details was not established.
- The 1577 Bungay/Blythburgh storm and demonic-dog narrative are later than 1540: possible later work, not a founding cause on that timeline. [Christian Owen's study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1467-9809.13099), abstract and indexed discussion, distinguishes the recorded storm from Fleming's polemical hound account.
- Famous later examples such as the eighteenth-century Beast of Gévaudan cannot motivate a branch founded in 1540 without deliberately altering chronology.
- The Black Death offers real scale but is a less distinctive fictional foundation; do not turn historical scapegoating of minority communities into validated supernatural causation.

**Possible placement for the fictional incident:** A brief revival during the 1539 inquiry leaves eyewitnesses with conflicting explanations. Their accounts could reach the authorities before the December questioning. This makes the event relevant to the proceedings without turning the March ceremony itself into a public demonstration. Keep the actual people accused distinct from the invented practitioners and patients. A variant in which the revival triggers the investigation would require placing it before the inquiry began and deliberately changing the documented cause.

The earlier living-volunteer treatment remains an exploratory antecedent. Whether limb animation and short revival contributed to lasting revival, and how the process became dependable in the field, remain open.

## Returning dead and corpse experiments, 1540–1600

**Fictional direction under discussion — Open.** After 1540, the researchers continue necromancy quietly. Their immediate objective is to use recently dead bodies in hazardous rescue work and as shock troops, drawing on the parent order's established duties of care and armed protection. These are intended applications. Dates of trials, successful deployment, consent, command and failure modes remain Open.

The sources below distinguish alleged event dates from when the account was recorded. They document stories, accusations and responses to suspected activity. Operational use of animated corpses belongs to the fictional research timeline.

The episode belongs to the preceding journey of 1528–1536. It is a published healing analogue for researchers after 1540.

**Fictional application — Open.** The Leipzig account offers an example of how people could describe active corpses and blame the people handling them. The Kelley story gives a direct analogue for trying to use a freshly dead body. Harriot's report gives an independent early American comparison about a person returning and speaking about death. Which sources the sect hears, whether a living mind returns during its experiments, and whether any historical episode intersects with those experiments remain unresolved.

## Recommendation and next decision — Open

Revenant accounts offer the best dangerous-folklore basis; holy-fire traditions offer the best connection to a hospital order; Paris's wolves offer the strongest concrete predatory crisis. Dancing outbreaks are especially close to the proposed date but have weaker secure mortality evidence.

Separate the date of the fictional branch's organization from the age of its knowledge. Older problems could have accumulated experience; a crisis near 1540 could explain formal cooperation when resources and authority were disrupted. No such earlier network, crisis, continuity or mechanism has been established.

Trace how the experiments might lead to the [operator milestones and early revivals](Order-and-Colonial-Company.md#revenant-operators--open). The 1539 incident, the results of the post-1540 rescue and combat experiments, and the exact date and condition of the first lasting revival remain open. Navarre and the English suppression can still serve separate functions in the branch's origin.
